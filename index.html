My ER Journey

Follow each step of your emergency department visit. Understanding what to expect can help reduce anxiety and help you know what's happening.

1

Check In

▼

What Happens

You arrive at the emergency department and go to the registration desk. Staff will ask for your insurance card, ID, and basic health information like allergies and current medications.

Why It Happens

The ER needs to know who you are, what your insurance is, and important health details to keep you safe and ensure they can contact you.

Common Questions

  • How long does check-in take?
  • What do I need to bring?
  • What if I don't have insurance?

⏱️ Typically 5-10 minutes

2

Triage

▼

What Happens

A nurse takes you to a private area and quickly assesses your condition. They will take your vital signs (temperature, blood pressure, heart rate, breathing rate), ask about your symptoms, and ask questions about your health history.

Why It Happens

Triage helps the ER team understand how sick or injured you are. This determines how urgently you need care, not the order you'll be seen (first come, first served).

Common Questions

  • Why do they ask so many questions?
  • Will the order I'm seen depend on when I arrived?
  • What if my condition changes while waiting?

⏱️ Typically 10-15 minutes

3

Waiting Room

▼

What Happens

After triage, you wait in the waiting room until a treatment room is available. The wait time depends on how busy the ER is, what type of patients are already being treated, and how serious their conditions are.

Why It Happens

ERs handle emergencies based on urgency, not the order patients arrive. Someone with a heart attack will be seen before someone with a sprained ankle, even if the ankle injury arrived first.

Common Questions

  • Why is the wait so long?
  • Can someone check on me?
  • What if I feel worse while waiting?

⏱️ Variable (15 minutes to several hours)

4

Treatment Room

▼

What Happens

You are taken to a treatment room or bed. A nurse or doctor will ask more detailed questions about your symptoms, perform a physical exam, and may place an IV if needed.

Why It Happens

The care team needs detailed information and needs to examine you directly to understand what's happening and what tests or treatment you might need.

Common Questions

  • Will it hurt?
  • Why do they need another exam?
  • What is an IV?

⏱️ Typically 20-45 minutes

5

Testing & Diagnosis

▼

What Happens

Based on your symptoms, the doctor may order tests like blood work, X-rays, ultrasounds, CT scans, or an ECG. These tests help the doctor understand what's causing your symptoms.

Why It Happens

Tests provide information the doctor cannot get from just talking to you or examining you. They help doctors make an accurate diagnosis.

Common Questions

  • Why do I need so many tests?
  • Is the radiation from X-rays safe?
  • How long do tests take?

⏱️ 30 minutes to several hours (depends on test type)

6

Results & Next Steps

▼

What Happens

The doctor reviews your test results and physical exam findings. They discuss what they found, explain your diagnosis, and talk about what happens next — whether you can go home, need more testing, need treatment, or need to be admitted to the hospital.

Why It Happens

Understanding your condition helps you know what to expect and what to do next to get better.

Common Questions

  • What do my test results mean?
  • Am I going to be okay?
  • Do I have to stay in the hospital?
7

Discharge

▼

What Happens

Before you leave, a nurse or doctor will give you discharge instructions. These will explain your diagnosis, what medications to take at home, what activities to avoid, and when to follow up with your doctor. You'll also get written instructions and may have questions answered.

Why It Happens

Discharge instructions help ensure you continue to recover properly at home and know when to return to the hospital if something changes.

Common Questions

  • What do I need to do at home?
  • When do I see my regular doctor?
  • When should I come back?

⏱️ Typically 15-20 minutes

💡 Remember: Every ER visit is unique. Your care team may prioritize differently based on your condition and the needs of other patients. Don't hesitate to ask your nurse or doctor questions about what's happening or what to expect next.

Ask ER Companion

Have questions about medical terms, tests, procedures, or how the ER works? Ask away—ER Companion is here to explain things in simple, clear language.

You can ask about: Medical terms, tests (blood work, X-rays, CT scans), procedures (stitches, IVs, casts), medications, ER workflows, and why you might need certain things done.

⚠️ Important: ER Companion explains things in general terms. For advice about YOUR specific situation, always talk to your healthcare team. In an emergency, call 911.

Welcome! I'm ER Companion. Ask me about medical terms, tests, procedures, ER processes, or anything else about the emergency department. What would you like to know?

💡 Tips for Asking

  • Be specific: "What does a CT scan do?" works better than "Tell me about tests."
  • Ask about processes: "How does triage work?" or "Why does the ER prioritize by urgency?"
  • Don't ask about your personal diagnosis: For that, talk to your doctor.
  • It's okay to follow up: Ask clarifying questions if something doesn't make sense.

Common ER Tests

Tests help doctors understand what's happening in your body. Here's what you might expect.

🩸 Blood Tests

Complete Blood Count (CBC)

▼

What is it?

A blood test that counts the different types of cells in your blood: red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets.

Why might a doctor order this?

Doctors order a CBC to check if you have an infection, anemia, bleeding problems, or certain diseases.

What happens?

A nurse or lab technician will take a small sample of blood from a vein in your arm using a needle. It takes about 30 seconds. The blood is then tested in the lab.

Common Questions

  • Will it hurt?
  • How long does it take?
  • When will I get results?
  • Why does the doctor need this test?

Comprehensive Metabolic Panel (CMP)

▼

What is it?

A blood test that measures 14 different chemicals in your blood to check kidney function, liver function, and electrolyte balance.

Why might a doctor order this?

Doctors use this to check if your kidneys and liver are working properly, and to see if your electrolytes (sodium, potassium) are balanced.

What happens?

A nurse takes a blood sample from your arm (like the CBC). No special preparation is needed. Results take a few hours.

Common Questions

  • What do these chemicals do?
  • Does this test hurt?
  • What if something is abnormal?

Troponin Test (Cardiac Marker)

▼

What is it?

A blood test that checks for a protein released by the heart when it's damaged. It helps doctors know if you've had a heart attack.

Why might a doctor order this?

If you have chest pain, shortness of breath, or heart-related symptoms, the doctor will order a troponin test.

What happens?

A blood sample is taken from your arm. The test is quick and painless. Results usually take 30-60 minutes.

Common Questions

  • Do I have a heart attack?
  • How accurate is this test?
  • Can this be normal if I had chest pain?

📸 Imaging Tests

X-Ray

▼

What is it?

An imaging test that uses a small amount of radiation to take a picture of the inside of your body. Common X-rays include chest, abdomen, or bone X-rays.

Why might a doctor order this?

Doctors use X-rays to look for broken bones, pneumonia, fluid in the lungs, or other abnormalities.

What happens?

You'll be positioned in front of an X-ray machine. The technician will take pictures from different angles. Each picture takes only a few seconds. You won't feel anything.

Common Questions

  • Is radiation dangerous?
  • Why do you have to step out of the room?
  • How long does it take?
  • Can pregnant women get X-rays?

CT (CAT) Scan

▼

What is it?

A special X-ray machine that takes many pictures from different angles and creates a detailed 3D image of your body. It can show soft tissues better than regular X-rays.

Why might a doctor order this?

Doctors use CT scans to look for internal injuries, bleeding, tumors, or other serious conditions. It's especially useful for head, chest, and abdominal injuries.

What happens?

You'll lie on a table that slides into a large, donut-shaped machine. You'll hear some noise. It takes 10-30 minutes depending on what area is being scanned. You won't feel anything.

Common Questions

  • Is a CT scan safe?
  • Why does it take so long?
  • Will I be alone in the machine?
  • Do I need contrast dye?

📋 About Your Results: Your doctor will explain what your test results mean. Test results are interpreted in context—one number means different things for different people and different conditions. Always ask your care team to explain any results you don't understand.

ER Procedures

Procedures are treatments or diagnostic steps. Knowing what to expect can help reduce anxiety.

IV (Intravenous) Placement

▼

What Happens

A nurse will clean a spot on your arm with an antiseptic wipe, then insert a small needle with a plastic tube (catheter) into a vein. The needle is removed, leaving just the plastic tube in place. This usually takes 30-60 seconds.

Why It's Done

An IV allows doctors and nurses to give you fluids, medications, or draw blood without repeated needle sticks. It's a quick way to get treatment into your body.

What You May Feel

You'll feel a quick pinch when the needle goes in. Once it's in, you shouldn't feel much except pressure. The area might feel a little sore for a moment.

Questions to Ask

  • Will it hurt?
  • How long will the IV stay in?
  • Can I move my arm with the IV in?
  • What if the nurse misses the vein?

Blood Draw (Phlebotomy)

▼

What Happens

A lab technician or nurse will tie a rubber band around your upper arm to make veins more visible, clean the area with an antiseptic wipe, and insert a needle to draw blood into vials. The whole process takes 2-3 minutes.

Why It's Done

Blood tests help doctors understand what's happening in your body by checking cells, chemicals, and markers of disease.

What You May Feel

You'll feel a pinch when the needle enters your skin. Some people feel lightheaded, which is normal. Tell the staff if you feel faint.

Questions to Ask

  • How many vials do you need?
  • When will I get results?
  • What if you miss the vein?
  • Why do I need so many tests?

Stitches (Sutures)

▼

What Happens

After the doctor cleans and inspects your wound, they may numb the area with anesthetic (so you won't feel pain, only pressure). Then they'll use a needle and thread to close the wound with several stitches. This takes 5-20 minutes depending on the size.

Why It's Done

Stitches hold your skin together while it heals, reduce scarring, and prevent infection.

What You May Feel

You'll feel pressure and tugging, but if numbed properly, you shouldn't feel sharp pain. Some people hear the needle go through their skin.

Questions to Ask

  • When do the stitches come out?
  • How do I care for this at home?
  • Will there be a scar?
  • What should I watch for?

Splint Application

▼

What Happens

After an X-ray to check for fractures, a nurse or doctor will apply a splint to immobilize your arm, leg, or other body part. They'll pad the area first, then wrap the splint securely. This takes 10-15 minutes.

Why It's Done

A splint prevents movement of the injured area, reduces pain, and helps prevent further injury while you heal.

What You May Feel

Pressure as the splint is applied. Once on, it should feel supportive and reduce pain. It might feel itchy or uncomfortable at first.

Questions to Ask

  • How long do I have to wear this?
  • Can I shower with it?
  • When do I need a real cast?
  • What exercises should I do?

💬 It's Okay to Ask: Before any procedure, you can ask: "Can you explain what you're about to do?" or "Is this going to hurt?" Your care team wants you to feel comfortable and informed.

Medication Categories

Here's general information about types of medications used in the ER. Ask your doctor or nurse about YOUR medications specifically.

⚠️ Important: This explains medication types in general. For questions about medications YOU are taking or about to take, always talk to your doctor or nurse.

💊 Pain Medications

Acetaminophen (Tylenol)

▼

What It Does

Reduces pain and fever by affecting how your body senses pain and regulates temperature.

Why It's Used

  • Headache
  • Fever
  • General body aches
  • Mild to moderate pain

Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin)

▼

What It Does

Reduces pain, fever, and inflammation (swelling) by decreasing chemicals in your body that cause these symptoms.

Why It's Used

  • Muscle strain
  • Joint pain
  • Fever
  • Inflammation
  • Menstrual cramps

🦠 Antibiotics

Antibiotics (Examples: Amoxicillin, Azithromycin)

▼

What It Does

Kill or slow down the growth of bacteria that are causing an infection.

Why It's Used

  • Bacterial infection
  • Pneumonia
  • Urinary tract infection
  • Strep throat
  • Wound infection

💧 IV Fluids

IV Fluids (Normal Saline, Lactated Ringer's)

▼

What It Does

Replace lost fluids and electrolytes in your body to correct dehydration.

Why It's Used

  • Dehydration
  • Severe vomiting or diarrhea
  • Blood loss
  • Shock
  • Before surgery

💬 Ask About Your Meds

Before taking any medication, it's good to ask:

  • What is this medication?
  • What will it do?
  • Are there side effects I should know about?
  • Do I take this at home, or only here?
  • Can I take this with my other medications?

Why Am I Waiting?

ERs can feel like long waits. Understanding how emergency departments prioritize care can help you understand why.

🚨 Urgency-Based Care, Not First-Come-First-Served

Emergency departments don't work like a restaurant or DMV. Instead of seeing patients in the order they arrive, ERs prioritize patients based on how urgently they need treatment. A patient having a heart attack will be seen before someone with a sprained ankle—even if the ankle injury arrived first.

📋 What is Triage?

When you arrive at the ER, a nurse performs "triage"—a quick assessment to determine how urgently you need care. Based on your symptoms, vital signs, and medical history, the triage nurse will assign you a priority level.

🔴 Level 1: Emergent (Immediate)

Life-threatening situations requiring immediate care. Examples: severe trauma, unconsciousness, active seizures, severe difficulty breathing.

🟠 Level 2: Urgent (Very Soon)

Serious but not immediately life-threatening. Examples: chest pain, severe head injury, difficulty breathing, loss of consciousness recovering, severe abdominal pain.

🟡 Level 3: Moderate (Relatively Soon)

Symptoms that need care but aren't immediately dangerous. Examples: moderate pain, minor injuries, fever, mild shortness of breath.

🟢 Level 4: Minor (Least Urgent)

Stable with minor problems. Examples: small cuts, sprains, mild rashes, cold symptoms.

⏳ Why Am I Still Waiting?

1. Higher-Priority Patients

▼

If you triage as Level 3 or 4 and there are Level 1 or 2 patients, they'll be seen first. That's the right call medically.

2. Limited Rooms & Staff

▼

ERs have a fixed number of treatment beds and doctors. If all beds are full, you wait for one to open. Staff are working as fast as they safely can.

3. Tests Take Time

▼

Lab work, imaging (X-rays, CT scans), and other tests don't give instant results. Doctors can't move forward until they have the information they need.

4. Unexpected Changes

▼

Another patient might suddenly become more critical, requiring the doctor's immediate attention. This is normal in emergency medicine.

Remember: Emergency department staff are working hard to care for all patients safely. Waits happen, but they reflect the complexity of emergency medicine and the commitment to prioritizing the sickest patients. Your care matters, and doctors will see you as soon as they can.

Medical Dictionary

Hear a word in the ER and don't know what it means? Look it up here.

🔍

Triage

▼

Simple Definition

The process nurses use to quickly check your condition and decide how urgently you need care.

Why Patients Hear It

The nurse will say "Let me triage you" or "You're next for triage" when you first arrive.

Example

"During triage, a nurse will ask about your symptoms and take your vital signs (temperature, blood pressure, heart rate)."

NPO

▼

Simple Definition

It means "nothing by mouth" — you cannot eat or drink anything.

Why Patients Hear It

Doctors use this before surgery, procedures, or if they're thinking you might need one.

Example

The nurse might say "The doctor ordered NPO because we might need to do a procedure." This usually means you should not have food or water.

STAT

▼

Simple Definition

It means "right now" or "immediately" — it's the urgency code doctors use.

Why Patients Hear It

When something needs to happen fast, the doctor will order it "STAT."

Example

A doctor might say "I want a chest X-ray STAT" which means the radiology team will drop other work to do it quickly.

IV

▼

Simple Definition

A small tube placed in your vein so doctors can give you fluids, medications, or draw blood without repeated needles.

Why Patients Hear It

Many ER patients get an IV as part of their care.

Example

A nurse might place an IV in your arm and connect it to a bag of fluids to help with dehydration or to have a line available for medication.

CBC

▼

Simple Definition

A blood test that counts your red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets to check your overall blood health.

Why Patients Hear It

CBC is one of the most common blood tests ordered in the ER.

Example

A doctor might order a CBC to check if you have an infection or anemia.

CT Scan

▼

Simple Definition

A special X-ray machine that takes many pictures from different angles and creates a detailed 3D image of your body.

Why Patients Hear It

Doctors use CT scans when they need to see inside your body in detail.

Example

After head trauma, a doctor might order a CT scan of your head to check for any injuries.

ECG (or EKG)

▼

Simple Definition

A simple test that records the electrical activity of your heart using stickers on your chest.

Why Patients Hear It

ECG is often done quickly in the ER if chest pain is a concern.

Example

The nurse will place small sticky pads on your chest for 30 seconds to record your heart rhythm.

Vital Signs

▼

Simple Definition

The basic measurements of your body's functions: temperature, blood pressure, heart rate, and breathing rate.

Why Patients Hear It

Nurses take vital signs constantly throughout your ER visit.

Example

"I'm going to check your vitals" means the nurse will take your temperature, check your pulse, and measure your blood pressure.

Admission

▼

Simple Definition

When you are approved to stay in the hospital for overnight care or longer.

Why Patients Hear It

After the ER, some patients are admitted to the hospital for continued treatment.

Example

The doctor might say "We're going to admit you to the hospital for observation and treatment."

Discharge

▼

Simple Definition

When the doctor says you can leave the hospital or ER because your condition is stable enough to continue care at home.

Why Patients Hear It

Discharge means you're leaving the ER or hospital.

Example

The nurse will say "The doctor cleared you for discharge" and provide you with instructions for home care.

💡 Tip: If you hear a medical term you don't understand, it's always okay to ask: "Can you explain what that means?" Healthcare providers expect questions and want you to understand.

Questions to Ask Your Care Team

Asking questions is a sign of good self-advocacy. Here are some questions that might help you understand your care better.

✨ Your Voice Matters: Good healthcare is a two-way conversation. Your doctors and nurses WANT to answer your questions. Don't worry about bothering them—you won't.

🧠 Understanding Questions

"What exactly is happening to me?"

▼

This helps ensure you understand your condition. Your care team should explain it in a way that makes sense to you.

"Can you explain that in simpler terms?"

▼

It's okay to ask for clarification. Medical terms can be confusing, and you deserve to understand your own care.

"Why do I need this test/procedure?"

▼

Understanding the reason for tests or procedures helps you know why they're important and what they're checking for.

👀 What to Expect

"What should I expect to happen next?"

▼

Knowing the next steps helps reduce anxiety and prepares you for what's coming.

"How long will this take?"

▼

Understanding timelines helps you mentally prepare and know what to expect.

"Will this hurt?"

▼

It's important to know what you might experience so there are no surprises.

🏥 Follow-Up Questions

"What should I do at home to take care of myself?"

▼

Your care team will give you discharge instructions. Ask if anything is unclear.

"When should I follow up with my doctor?"

▼

Follow-up appointments are important for your recovery. Make sure you know when and where to go.

"When should I come back to the ER?"

▼

You should know the red flags that mean you need to return immediately.

💬 How to Ask Effectively

  • Be direct: "Can you explain that differently?"
  • Bring support: A family member can help you remember
  • Write things down: Jot down what was said
  • Ask for written info: Request printed instructions
  • Ask again: It's totally okay to ask follow-up questions

Parent Resources

Taking a child to the ER can be stressful. Here's information to help you support your child and understand common pediatric ER situations.

❤️ You're Doing Great: Bringing your child to the ER shows you care about their health. Staff understand that parents are often anxious too. Take a breath—your calm presence helps your child.

Common Pediatric Situations

Understanding Fever in Children

▼

Content

Fever is your child's body's way of fighting infection. A fever itself is not dangerous, though it can make your child feel uncomfortable. In the ER, doctors check fever along with other symptoms to understand what's happening.

Tips

  • Fever alone is usually not an emergency unless accompanied by difficulty breathing, unresponsiveness, or severe symptoms
  • Keep your child hydrated with water, juice, or oral rehydration solutions
  • Light clothing and cool environments help comfort
  • Over-the-counter fever reducers (like acetaminophen or ibuprofen) can be given as directed

Preparing Your Child for Stitches

▼

Content

If your child needs stitches, the doctor will numb the area so they won't feel pain, only pressure and tugging. Explain this simply: "The doctor is going to clean the cut and put special thread to help it heal. You'll feel pushing but not owies."

Tips

  • Use simple, honest language. Avoid words like "shot" or "needle" — say "medicine to help it not hurt"
  • Let them know they might hear or feel pressure but it shouldn't hurt
  • Bring a comfort item or have them hold a parent's hand
  • Praise brave behavior afterward, but don't make a big deal out of it

Helping Your Anxious Child in the ER

▼

Content

The ER can be scary for children. Your calm presence and honest explanations help them feel safer.

Tips

  • Stay calm — children sense your anxiety
  • Explain things before they happen: "A nurse is going to check your temperature. It will feel cool on your forehead."
  • Use distraction: favorite stuffed animal, music, or games on a phone
  • Validate their feelings: "I know you're scared. The doctors are going to help you feel better."
  • Ask staff if your child can see what's being used before it's used

General Tips for Parents in the ER

  • Stay as calm as possible: Children pick up on parental anxiety
  • Be honest: Don't say "it won't hurt" if a procedure will be uncomfortable
  • Use simple language: Explain procedures in age-appropriate terms
  • Bring comfort items: A favorite stuffed animal or blanket can be very soothing
  • Advocate for your child: Ask questions and speak up if your child is in pain
  • Ask about what's next: Children feel less anxious when they know what to expect
  • Praise bravery: After procedures, let your child know you're proud of them

📚 After the ER: Talk with your child about what happened. This helps process the experience and reduce anxiety about future medical visits. If your child seems to have ongoing anxiety or trauma from the visit, talk to your pediatrician about support options.

Safety & Disclaimer

Understanding what ER Companion can and cannot do is important for your safety.

🚫 ER Companion Does NOT

  • Diagnose your condition or tell you what is wrong with you
  • Interpret your test results or lab values
  • Recommend specific treatments or medications for your individual situation
  • Provide personalized medical advice
  • Replace doctors, nurses, or other healthcare professionals
  • Make clinical decisions for you

✓ ER Companion DOES

  • Explain common medical terms and ER processes in simple language
  • Help you understand what is happening during your ER visit
  • Describe common tests, procedures, and medications in general terms
  • Answer questions about how the ER works
  • Provide general health education
  • Help you prepare questions to ask your care team
  • Support your health literacy and understanding

⚖️ Important Disclaimer

ER Companion is an educational tool designed to help patients and families understand the emergency department experience and medical concepts. It is not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Always rely on your healthcare team for personalized medical guidance. If you have emergency symptoms, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department immediately.

🚨 Call 911 If You Have

• Chest pain or pressure

• Severe difficulty breathing

• Loss of consciousness

• Severe bleeding

• Severe allergic reaction

• Suspected poisoning or overdose

• Suicidal thoughts or self-harm

• Severe injury from trauma

• Any other life-threatening emergency

💬 For Personalized Medical Advice

Always talk to your healthcare team. Your doctor, nurse, or other healthcare providers know your medical history and can give you personalized advice. ER Companion provides general education only.

Share ER Companion with your team. If you find ER Companion helpful, share it with your healthcare providers. They may want to recommend it to other patients.
